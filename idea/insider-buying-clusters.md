Insider Buying Clusters — Strategy Document


WHAT IS AN INSIDER?

An "insider" in SEC terms is anyone with access to material non-public information about a company:
* CEO, CFO, COO, and other C-suite executives
* Board of directors
* Officers (VP-level and above)
* Any shareholder owning > 10% of the company

These people are legally required to report every purchase or sale of their own company's stock to the SEC within 2 business days via Form 4.


WHAT IS CLUSTERED INSIDER BUYING?

A single insider buying stock could mean anything — maybe they're diversifying into their own company, maybe it's part of a compensation plan, maybe they just got a bonus and parked it.

But when 3 or more insiders at the same company independently buy open-market shares with their own money within a 60-90 day window, that's a cluster. It means multiple people who know the business intimately — who see the pipeline, the contracts, the margins, the trajectory — all independently decided "this stock is too cheap." They're not coordinating (that would be illegal). They're each, separately, looking at the same internal reality and reaching the same conclusion: this is undervalued.

The key distinction: open-market purchases only. Ignore:
* Stock option exercises (these are compensation, not conviction)
* Automatic purchase plans (10b5-1 plans — pre-scheduled, no signal)
* Gifts or transfers
Only count direct open-market buys where the insider chose to spend their own cash.

Critical filter — routine vs. opportunistic (Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski, 2012):
Not all open-market purchases are equal. Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski found that insiders who buy at roughly the same time every year (same month, similar size) are "routine" buyers — they're on a personal allocation schedule, not expressing conviction. Their trades predict nothing. Returns are statistically zero.

"Opportunistic" buyers are the ones who break their own pattern — they buy at irregular intervals, at unusual sizes, often after price declines. These trades predict 6-month excess returns of ~5-8%. The distinction is simple: if an insider bought in March every year for the last 4 years, their March purchase this year isn't a signal. If they haven't bought in 3 years and suddenly drop $2M in, that's a signal.

Our system must track each insider's personal buying history and flag only pattern-breaking purchases.


WHY THIS WORKS

Insiders have a legal information advantage that no filing or screen can replicate. They know:
* Whether the sales pipeline is filling or drying up
* Whether a major customer is about to renew or leave
* Whether the cost structure is improving or deteriorating
* Whether the next quarter's earnings will surprise

They can't trade on specific material non-public information (that's insider trading, illegal). But they can trade on their general confidence in the business. When multiple insiders simultaneously express that confidence with real money, the signal is strong.


THE SIGNALING PROBLEM — AND WHY CLUSTERS SOLVE IT

This is the most important nuance in this entire strategy.

Not all insider buying is genuine conviction. Chen & Li (2023) documented that a significant portion of insider purchases are strategic signaling — management buying their own stock specifically to project confidence to external investors, support the stock price during a decline, or counter negative narratives (including short seller reports). These "performative" purchases look identical to conviction purchases on a Form 4 filing.

How to tell the difference:
* Performative buying tends to be small relative to the insider's net worth (symbolic amounts)
* It often happens right after a stock decline, right before an earnings call, or in response to a short report — timed for maximum visibility
* It's usually just the CEO or one officer (a solo act, not a chorus)
* The insider may have a pattern of buying small amounts after every dip, which becomes routine

Cluster buying defeats the signaling problem because:
* It's much harder for 3-5 people to independently coordinate performative buying without crossing into illegal territory (coordinated insider action)
* When the CFO, a VP of operations, and two independent directors all separately buy within 60 days, they're each responding to internal reality — not performing for the market
* The cost of performative buying scales linearly with participants. One CEO buying $50K as a signal is cheap. Getting 4 insiders to each spend $200K+ just to signal is expensive and unlikely unless they genuinely believe
* Cluster buying after a 25-40% price decline is the strongest sub-signal: insiders are spending real money to buy more of something the market just punished them for owning

Bottom line: single insider purchases should be treated with skepticism. Clusters of 3+ should be treated as genuine conviction signals, especially when they pass the opportunistic filter.


THE EVIDENCE

Foundational studies (still directionally valid):
* Lakonishok & Lee (2001): Insider purchases at small-cap companies predicted 6-month excess returns of ~7%.
* Jeng, Metrick & Zeckhauser (2003): Insider purchase portfolios outperformed by ~6% annually.
* Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski (2012): Introduced the routine vs. opportunistic distinction. Only opportunistic trades predict returns. Routine trades earn zero. This single filter eliminates ~40% of insider purchases and dramatically improves signal quality.
* Studies focusing specifically on CLUSTERED buying (3+ insiders) show 10-15% annual outperformance over 12 months.

Recent evidence (2020-2026):
* Jiang, Ma & Ma (2025): COVID-era insider trading data (2020-2021) strongly validated the signal. During peak uncertainty, insiders who bought aggressively in March-April 2020 — when everyone else was panic-selling — captured 40-80% returns over the next 12 months. The signal works best precisely when markets are most dislocated.
* Das (2025): Confirmed that insider buying during the COVID crash outperformed across every sector, with the strongest results in the smallest companies.
* Ozlen & Batumoglu (2025) — "The Death of Insider Trading Alpha": Found that 70-80% of insider trading alpha dissipates BEFORE the Form 4 is publicly filed (within the 2-business-day window). This sounds devastating, but it has a specific implication: the alpha loss is worst for LARGE-CAP insiders whose trades leak through market microstructure (bid-ask movements, unusual volume). For SMALL/MICRO-CAP insiders with low liquidity, the alpha persists because there's no market microstructure to leak through. This REINFORCES our focus on the $50M-$500M universe.
* Oenschlager & Mollenhoff (2025): Returns from insider buying strategies diminish significantly when limited to realistic position sizes and accounting for transaction costs. But again, this applies to strategies that try to replicate insider portfolios at scale — not to using insider buying as a SIGNAL to inform a concentrated portfolio (which is what we do).
* Chen & Li (2023): Documented that many insider purchases are performative signaling, not genuine conviction. (See "The Signaling Problem" section above for how cluster buying addresses this.)
* Li, Mukherjee & Sen (2024): Social media discussion of insider trades does NOT crowd out the signal. Paradoxically, when insider purchases get discussed on Reddit/Twitter, the stock gets more volatile, which creates better entry points for those who've already identified the cluster. Social noise helps rather than hurts.
* Zhao (2026): Applied machine learning to microcap insider purchasing patterns and achieved AUC of 0.70 (reasonably predictive). The most important features: cluster count, purchase-to-salary ratio, days since last insider purchase, and analyst coverage (fewer analysts = stronger signal). Confirms that the features we already track are the right ones.

The real-money test — and why it matters:
* The Catalyst Insider Buying Fund (INSAX) is the only mutual fund that systematically follows insider buying signals. Its 10-year annualized return: -0.52%. It underperformed the S&P 500 by ~12% annually.
* Why? Because it buys EVERY insider purchase signal without filtering for clusters, without the routine/opportunistic filter, without a valuation screen, and across ALL market caps (including large-caps where alpha decays fastest). It proves that raw insider buying data, applied naively, does not work.
* Our approach is fundamentally different: we filter for clusters (3+), filter for opportunistic timing, filter for small/micro-cap only, cross-reference with a valuation screen, and use it as one signal among many — not a standalone strategy. The academic evidence says this filtered approach works. The unfiltered approach provably doesn't.

The signal is strongest at small/micro-cap companies with low analyst coverage — exactly the zone this system operates in.
The signal is weakest at large caps with heavy coverage, because the information asymmetry is smaller and alpha decays before filing.


REGULATORY CHANGES: 10b5-1 PLAN REFORM (2022)

In December 2022, the SEC amended Rule 10b5-1, which governs pre-planned insider trading programs. Key changes effective February 2023:
* 90-day cooling-off period: Insiders must wait 90 days after adopting or modifying a 10b5-1 plan before the first trade executes. Previously there was no mandatory cooling-off, so insiders could adopt a "plan" and trade immediately — effectively laundering informed trades as "pre-planned."
* One plan at a time: Insiders can no longer maintain multiple overlapping plans (previously used to cherry-pick the most favorable one).
* No single-trade plans: Plans designed to execute a single trade are no longer shielded. This eliminates the most egregious abuse.
* Certification requirement: Insiders must certify they're not aware of MNPI when adopting a plan.
* Disclosure on Form 4: Companies must now disclose whether a trade was made under a 10b5-1 plan, making it easier to distinguish planned vs. discretionary trades.

What this means for our system:
* The 10b5-1 reforms HELP us. Before the reform, some "open-market" purchases were actually pre-planned and carried no signal. Now the filing explicitly marks 10b5-1 trades, so our filter (open-market only, no 10b5-1) is more accurate.
* The reforms also reduced the total volume of insider trading (because the abusive shortcuts were closed), which means the remaining open-market purchases are more likely to be genuine conviction signals.


EDGE

The data is free and public. Everyone CAN see it. But almost nobody systematically monitors insider buying across the entire small/micro-cap universe in real-time. Most investors check insider buying after they're already interested in a stock. The edge is:
1. Monitoring every Form 4 filed daily across the entire market
2. Automatically detecting cluster patterns (3+ insiders, 60-90 day window, open-market only, opportunistic only)
3. Tracking each insider's personal buying history to distinguish routine from opportunistic behavior
4. Cross-referencing clusters with the quantitative screen — a company that is BOTH statistically cheap AND seeing insider cluster buying is the intersection of two independent signals
5. Layering the confidence model — insider buying boosts P_mgmt and P_real significantly, because if insiders are buying, they believe the earnings are real and the business is sound
6. Operating exclusively in the $50M-$500M universe where alpha decay is minimal and information asymmetry is highest


APPROACH

Step 1: Daily Form 4 ingestion
* Pull every Form 4 filed with the SEC daily
* Parse for: insider name, title, company (CIK/ticker), transaction type, shares purchased, price paid, date
* Check the 10b5-1 flag: if the trade was executed under a 10b5-1 plan, discard it

Step 2: Filter for open-market purchases only
* Discard option exercises, 10b5-1 plan transactions, gifts, private transactions
* Transaction code "P" = open-market purchase (this is the one you want)

Step 3: Routine vs. opportunistic classification
* For each insider, maintain a history of all prior purchases (dates, sizes, timing relative to fiscal calendar)
* Flag as ROUTINE if the purchase follows the insider's historical pattern (same quarter, similar size, recurring annually)
* Flag as OPPORTUNISTIC if the purchase breaks pattern: first buy in 12+ months, unusual size, follows a price decline, or no prior buying history
* Discard routine purchases from the cluster count

Step 4: Cluster detection
* For each company, maintain a rolling 90-day window of OPPORTUNISTIC insider purchases
* Flag when 3+ distinct insiders have opportunistic open-market purchases within the window
* Weight by: purchase size relative to insider's annual compensation (>50% = strong), insider's title (CEO/CFO > VP > director), and whether the purchase follows a price decline of 15%+

Step 5: Cross-reference with valuation screen
* Run the flagged company through the quantitative screen
* Is it cheap on P/B, EV/EBIT, FCF yield?
* Does it pass kill criteria?
* What's the confidence composite?

Step 6: Cross-reference with other signal layers
* Check if any super investors (13F filers) have recently initiated or added to a position in the same company
* Check if any activist investors have filed a 13D on the company (or its sector peers)
* Check congressional trading disclosures for committee-relevant trades in the same sector
* Check social/demand signals for the company's products (search trends, app downloads, web traffic)
* The highest-conviction trades are when multiple independent signal layers converge on the same company

Step 7: Context enrichment
* Pull recent news — why might insiders be buying? Post-earnings dip? Sector panic? Post-spin-off?
* Check if the company is in any other anomaly pipeline (spin-off, post-bankruptcy, forced-selling event)
* Read the most recent 10-K MD&A for tone and outlook

Output: A ranked list of companies with insider cluster buying, sorted by composite confidence score.


COMPLEMENTARY SIGNALS: ACTIVIST INVESTORS (13D) AND SUPER INVESTORS (13F)

Insider buying clusters don't exist in isolation. Two other categories of informed buyers file with the SEC, and their signals complement the Form 4 data:

Activist investors (13D filings):
When an investor acquires 5%+ of a company's shares with the intent to influence management, they must file a Schedule 13D within 10 business days. This filing is a strong signal because:
* The investor has done deep due diligence before committing significant capital
* They have a specific thesis for how to unlock value (cost cuts, asset sales, board changes, strategic review)
* The announcement effect alone averages 7-8% on the filing date
* Post-announcement drift adds another 2-5% over the following months as the market digests the implications
* The investor is publicly committed — they can't quietly exit without filing an amendment

How to use 13D signals in our system:
* Monitor all 13D filings daily (SEC EDGAR provides these)
* Flag when a credible activist (see the informed-traders-watchlist.json for the list) files a 13D on a company in our $50M-$500M universe
* Cross-reference with our valuation screen: if the company was already flagged as cheap AND an activist just showed up, that's convergence
* If the company also has insider cluster buying happening simultaneously, that's triple convergence — the insiders and the activist both see the same undervaluation

Super investors (13F filings):
Large investment managers ($100M+ AUM) must file quarterly 13F reports disclosing their equity holdings. The filing deadline is 45 days after quarter end. This creates a lag, but for concentrated, low-turnover investors (who hold positions for years), the lag doesn't matter much.

How to use 13F signals in our system:
* Track 13F filings from the highest-conviction investors (see the informed-traders-watchlist.json for the list)
* Flag when a top-tier investor initiates a new position or significantly increases an existing one
* Weight new positions more heavily than position increases (new = fresh conviction)
* The most useful 13F signals come from concentrated managers who hold 8-15 stocks — their new positions represent high conviction
* Cross-reference with our valuation screen and insider buying clusters

When multiple independent signals converge:
The single most powerful setup in this system is when the following happen simultaneously or within a short window:
1. A company passes the quantitative value screen (cheap on fundamentals)
2. 3+ insiders are cluster-buying (opportunistic, not routine)
3. A credible activist has filed a 13D OR a top super investor has initiated a position
4. Congressional members on a relevant committee are buying the sector
5. Social/demand signals show improving trends for the company's products

Any 3 of these 5 converging on the same company is an extremely high-conviction setup. All 5 converging is vanishingly rare — but when it happens, it's the kind of opportunity the entire system is designed to find.


MULTI-SIGNAL CONVERGENCE SCORING

For every company flagged by the insider cluster detection, compute a convergence score:

Base score (insider cluster):
* 3 insiders in 90-day window: +30 points
* 4 insiders: +45 points
* 5+ insiders: +60 points

Insider quality multipliers:
* CEO or CFO among buyers: +15 points
* Average purchase > 50% of annual compensation: +10 points
* Cluster follows 20%+ price decline: +10 points
* At least one insider breaking a 2+ year buying silence: +10 points
* Company has 0-1 sell-side analysts: +10 points

Cross-signal convergence:
* Passes quantitative value screen (P/B < 1, EV/EBIT < 6): +25 points
* Top-tier super investor initiated position in last 2 quarters: +20 points
* Activist 13D filed in last 6 months: +25 points
* Congressional committee members buying same sector: +10 points
* Social/demand signals above 2-sigma baseline: +10 points

Score interpretation:
* 30-50: Monitor — insider cluster detected but limited convergence
* 50-80: Interesting — insider cluster with some cross-signal support
* 80-120: Strong — multiple independent signals converging
* 120+: Highest conviction — rare, but when it happens, size the position accordingly

SIGNAL STRENGTH MODIFIERS

Stronger signal:
* CEO or CFO buying (they have the deepest knowledge)
* Purchase size > 50% of insider's annual compensation (meaningful skin in the game)
* Buying during or immediately after a price decline of 15%+ (buying the dip with conviction)
* First insider purchase in 2+ years (breaking a long silence — this is the "opportunistic" signal)
* Company has low analyst coverage, 0-2 analysts (information asymmetry is highest)
* Purchase is NOT under a 10b5-1 plan (discretionary, not pre-planned)
* Insider has a history of profitable past purchases at this company (track record of conviction accuracy)
* SELL signals from insiders at competitor companies simultaneously (insiders across the industry see the same trend — one company is winning, competitors know it)

Weaker signal:
* Only directors buying (less operational knowledge)
* Small purchases relative to net worth (token amounts — possible signaling, not conviction)
* Buying during a buyback program (may be signaling to support the program, not personal conviction)
* Company is large-cap with heavy coverage (insiders know less that the market doesn't already know, and alpha decays fastest here)
* Insider has a pattern of buying at the same time every year (routine buyer — no signal)
* Insider bought right after a short seller report (likely a defensive/performative purchase)


WHAT TO WATCH OUT FOR

* The signaling problem: Not all insider buying is genuine conviction. Some insiders buy small amounts to project confidence. Cluster buying (3+) and the opportunistic filter are the primary defenses against this. Single insider purchases should be treated with much lower confidence.
* Insider buying right before a known catalyst (earnings, FDA decision, contract announcement) could be illegal insider trading. If the cluster happens suspiciously close to a major announcement, that's a red flag, not a green flag.
* Some insiders buy routinely as part of their personal allocation strategy. Our system classifies each insider as routine or opportunistic and only counts opportunistic purchases toward clusters.
* Insider buying doesn't prevent short-term declines. The stock can still drop 20% after insiders buy. The signal is about 6-18 month forward returns, not next-week price action.
* Alpha decay is real at large caps. The Ozlen & Batumoglu (2025) finding — 70-80% of alpha dissipates before filing — is mostly a large-cap problem. In our $50M-$500M universe, the low liquidity means the insider's purchase doesn't move the market, and the Form 4 gets less attention, so the alpha persists longer.
* Don't try to replicate insider portfolios at scale. The Catalyst fund (INSAX) proved that a naive "buy everything insiders buy" approach fails. The signal works as a filter to identify high-conviction ideas within a pre-screened universe, not as a standalone strategy.
* The 45-day lag on 13F filings means super investor positions are stale by the time you see them. This is fine for concentrated, low-turnover managers (they hold for years), but worthless for high-turnover traders. Only track investors whose style makes the lag irrelevant.
