Congressional Trading Signals — Strategy Document


THE THESIS

Members of Congress have access to non-public information about upcoming legislation, regulatory actions, government contracts, and policy shifts. They sit in closed-door briefings, meet with lobbyists, draft bills that affect specific industries, and oversee agencies that regulate specific companies.

They are also allowed to trade stocks.

The STOCK Act (2012) requires them to disclose trades within 45 days, but it did not prohibit trading in companies their committees oversee. The result: a documented, statistically significant pattern of congressional members outperforming the market, particularly in industries their committees regulate.

This is the insider buying clusters thesis applied to political insiders instead of corporate insiders. Corporate insiders know the company's future. Congressional insiders know the industry's future.


THE EVIDENCE

Academic studies (chronological):

* Ziobrowski, Cheng, Boyd & Ziobrowski (2004): US Senators' stock portfolios outperformed the market by ~12% annually from 1993-1998. Did NOT distinguish leadership vs rank-and-file.
* Ziobrowski et al. (2011): House members outperformed by ~6% annually (1985-2001). Analyzed 16,000+ transactions by ~300 members. Also did NOT separate leadership from rank-and-file.
* Eggers & Hainmueller (2013, rebuttal): Argued the average member of Congress would have earned higher returns in a passive index fund (2004-2008). The "mediocre average" finding is actually consistent with a small informed minority generating alpha while the majority drags the average down.
* Karadas (2019): "Trading on Private Information" — Financial Review. Analyzed 61,998 transactions (2004-2010). Found "powerful members'" (committee chairs, subcommittee chairs, ranking members, party leadership) buy-minus-sell portfolios earned abnormal returns exceeding 35% annually. KEY FINDING: committee power, not just top leadership, drove outperformance. Abnormal returns disappeared after the STOCK Act (2012).
* Karadas (2018): "Family Ties and Informed Trading" — analyzed 22,159 transactions (2004-2010). Spouses of powerful politicians earned annualized abnormal returns exceeding 12%. Spouses of non-powerful politicians earned nothing. Spousal outperformance disappeared after the STOCK Act, confirming it was driven by shared non-public information.
* Molk & Partnoy (2024): "Negative Trading in Congress" — Indiana Law Journal. Found sell/short signals from Congress members ARE associated with positive abnormal returns, but long positions are NOT. Members "on average suffer modest losses" on buys. Sell signals are stronger than buy signals.
* Wei & Zhou (2025): "Captain Gains on Capitol Hill" — NBER Working Paper 34524. Leaders' trades outperform matched peers by 40-50 percentage points annually after ascension. BUT sample is only 20 lawmakers — statistically significant but small sample. Higher returns when their party controls the chamber. Sales precede adverse regulatory actions within 3-12 months.
* Ozlen & Batumoglu (2025): 70-80% of total alpha dissipates between transaction date and following trading day, well before public disclosure. Measured on Form 4 filings (2-day delay) — decay is even WORSE for congressional trades (45-day delay).
* Pyun (2025): Returns are higher from transaction date vs. reported date, but "a positive drift from both starting points suggests acting on reported trades is still timely enough to generate gains." Stronger for large-cap stocks and House members.

Key synthesis: The leadership-only thesis (Wei/Zhou) is directionally correct but overly narrow. The signal is actually: INFORMATION ACCESS + TRADING PATTERN + TRACK RECORD. A freshman on the Capital Markets subcommittee who times Oracle before TikTok is higher-signal than a Senate Majority Leader who doesn't trade.

Empirical data (2025):
* Unusual Whales 2025 Report: Only 32.2% of Congress beat the S&P 500. Democrats averaged 14.4%, Republicans 17.3%, S&P returned 16.6%.
* 140 members made 14,451 trades worth $720M in 2025.
* NANC/KRUZ ETFs (tracking ALL members indiscriminately) show middling risk-adjusted performance — confirming unweighted approaches dilute alpha.
* Quiver Quantitative's Congress Buys Strategy: 34.97% CAGR since inception, outperforming market — but this strategy filters for characteristics, not just tracks everyone.
* The 2025 top performers were a MIX of leadership and rank-and-file — Davidson (#1, buy-and-hold), Norcross (#2, Armed Services subcommittee RM), Sewell (#3, Ways & Means subcommittee RM), Steil (#4, House Admin Chair).
* The "Pelosi effect" is documented: stocks jump on average 2-5% in the days after her trade disclosures become public. But she is the most crowded signal — her alpha is partially priced in by copy-traders.


WHY COPYING PELOSI SPECIFICALLY DOESN'T WORK

1. She's the most watched member. Thousands of people use Pelosi trackers. The moment her disclosure is public, the stock moves. You're buying into the spike, not before it.
2. The 45-day disclosure delay. By the time you know about the trade, it could be 6 weeks old. The thesis behind the trade may already be partially or fully priced in.
3. Cherry-picking: The PayPal and Visa examples are her wins. She also has losing trades that don't make headlines. Selection bias in the narrative makes her look more prescient than her full portfolio suggests.
4. Crowded signal = no edge. When everyone copies the same person, the copies cancel out the advantage.


WHERE THE REAL EDGE IS

The edge is NOT Pelosi. The edge is the other 534 members of Congress that almost nobody tracks systematically.

Committee-specific trades:
The most powerful signal is when a member trades a stock in an industry their committee directly oversees. This is where the information asymmetry is strongest.

* Armed Services Committee member buys defense contractor stock → may know about upcoming contract awards or budget increases.
* Energy Committee member buys oil/gas stock → may know about upcoming regulatory relief or policy shifts.
* Finance/Banking Committee member sells bank stocks → may know about upcoming regulations or enforcement actions.
* Health Committee member buys pharma stock → may know about upcoming FDA decisions or Medicare policy changes.
* Intelligence Committee member makes any trade → they have access to classified briefings on geopolitical events.

The signal is: does the member's trade align with their committee's jurisdiction? If yes, the information asymmetry is high and the trade is potentially informed by non-public legislative/regulatory knowledge.

Non-obvious members:
* Junior members of Congress who attract zero media attention but sit on powerful committees.
* Senators from small states with minimal press coverage.
* Members who rarely trade but suddenly make a large, concentrated bet — the infrequency makes it a stronger signal (similar to insider cluster buying logic).

Cluster detection (same logic as corporate insider clusters):
* When 3+ members of the same committee trade in the same sector within a 30-day window, that's a political insider cluster. It suggests committee-level information is driving the trades.
* Example: If 4 members of the Senate Finance Committee all buy insurance stocks in the same month, something is happening in insurance regulation that hasn't been made public yet.


THE DISCLOSURE DELAY PROBLEM AND HOW TO WORK AROUND IT

The 45-day window is the main obstacle. Here's how to mitigate it:

1. Track actual disclosure speed, not the legal maximum.
* Many members disclose within 7-15 days, not 45. Build a database of each member's typical disclosure lag. Focus on the ones who disclose fast.
* Some disclose the same week as the trade. These are the most actionable.

2. Periodic Transaction Reports (PTRs) vs. Annual Disclosures.
* PTRs are filed throughout the year (the 45-day filings). These are timely.
* Annual Financial Disclosures are filed once per year with more detail.
* Focus on PTRs for timely signals.

3. Monitor for pre-disclosure price action.
* If a stock starts moving before the congressional trade is disclosed, others may have the same information. The trade is less useful.
* If the stock has NOT moved by the disclosure date, the market hasn't priced in whatever the member knows. This is where the remaining edge lives.

4. Combine with other signals.
* Congressional buy + insider cluster buy + cheap on value screen = triple confirmation from three independent sources.
* If a Health Committee member buys a pharma stock AND the company's executives are also buying AND the stock is cheap on fundamentals, the convergence of political + corporate + quantitative signals is very strong.


APPROACH

Step 1: Ingest all congressional trade disclosures
* Pull every Periodic Transaction Report (PTR) filed by all members of Congress.
* Parse: member name, state, party, trade date, disclosure date, stock ticker, transaction type (buy/sell), amount range, asset type.

Step 2: Map members to committees
* Maintain a mapping of every member of Congress to their committee assignments.
* Tag each trade as "committee-relevant" or "not committee-relevant" based on whether the traded company falls within the member's committee jurisdiction.

Step 3: Compute disclosure lag
* For each trade: disclosure lag = disclosure date − trade date.
* Track each member's average disclosure lag. Prioritize members with short lags (< 15 days).

Step 4: Cluster detection
* Same-member cluster: A single member making 3+ trades in the same sector within 30 days (concentrated bet).
* Committee cluster: 3+ members of the same committee trading in the same sector within 30 days (shared information signal).
* Bipartisan cluster: Members from BOTH parties on the same committee making similar trades. If a Democrat and a Republican on the Finance Committee both buy bank stocks, the signal is even stronger — it's not partisan positioning, it's shared non-public information.

Step 5: Cross-reference with the system
* Run committee-relevant, clustered, or fast-disclosed trades through:
  * Valuation screen (moody.md) — is the stock fundamentally cheap?
  * Insider buying clusters — are corporate insiders also buying?
  * Social signals — is there a demand shift visible in public data?
  * News layer — is there pending legislation or regulatory action that would explain the trade?
* Score using the confidence model. Congressional signal feeds into P_catalyst (political actors may be positioning ahead of a regulatory catalyst).

Step 6: Ignore the noise
* Discard: Trades in broad index funds, ETFs, or mega-cap stocks (these are generic portfolio allocation, not informed bets).
* Discard: Trades by members who trade frequently and diversely (they're just actively managing a portfolio, not trading on information).
* Focus on: Concentrated bets in specific companies, especially small/mid-caps, by members whose committee jurisdiction aligns with the trade.

MEMBER TIER SYSTEM — EVIDENCE-BASED WEIGHTING

Updated February 2026 based on cross-referencing 7 academic studies (Ziobrowski 2004/2011, Eggers & Hainmueller 2013, Karadas 2018/2019, Molk & Partnoy 2024, Wei & Zhou 2025) plus empirical 2025 performance data from Unusual Whales, Capitol Trades, and CNN investigations.

The original Columbia/CEPR thesis (Wei/Zhou 2025) — that only top leadership outperforms — was based on a sample of 20 lawmakers. While directionally correct, our empirical research across 103 members found that ~65% of committee chairs/ranking members don't trade stocks at all. The signal concentrates in a small minority defined by INFORMATION ACCESS + TRADING PATTERN + TRACK RECORD, not formal title alone.

Key empirical findings that shaped this system:
* 65-70% of committee leadership doesn't trade — title alone is not predictive.
* Karadas (2019) showed "committee power" (chairs, subcommittee chairs, ranking members) drives returns, not just top party leadership.
* Karadas (2018) showed spousal trades of powerful members earned 12%+ annualized abnormal returns. Spouses of non-powerful members earned zero.
* Molk & Partnoy (2024) showed sell signals outperform buy signals from Congress.
* 2025 data showed freshmen (Fields, Shreve, Bresnahan) can generate the highest-signal trades when they sit on key subcommittees.
* The highest-signal member in 2025 was Rep. Cleo Fields — a freshman on the Capital Markets subcommittee, not a party leader.

Tier 1 — Maximum weight (3x multiplier on signal strength):
Members who meet TWO OR MORE of: (a) committee/subcommittee chair or ranking member, (b) documented outperformance (>20% above S&P in any year), (c) trades with direct committee-jurisdiction overlap, (d) party leadership position (#1-#5 in party hierarchy).
* When their party controls the chamber, multiply by an additional 1.25x (Wei/Zhou finding).
* Their sells are weighted 1.5x higher than buys (Molk & Partnoy finding).
* Their spousal trades get equal weight to their own (Karadas 2018 finding).

Tier 2 — High weight (2x multiplier):
Members who meet ONE of: (a) subcommittee chair/ranking on a "Big 6" committee (Appropriations, Ways and Means/Finance, Armed Services, Intelligence, Financial Services, Energy & Commerce), (b) demonstrated consistent outperformance across 2+ years, (c) former senior government official (e.g., former Under Secretary of Defense, former hedge fund CEO) now on relevant committee, (d) high trade volume with clear sector concentration matching committee jurisdiction.

Tier 3 — Standard weight (1x multiplier):
Members who trade in their committee's jurisdiction but lack the track record or position of Tier 1-2. Includes freshmen in their monitoring period (first 1-2 years before track record is established). Worth monitoring for pattern development.

Tier 4 — Low weight (0.5x multiplier):
Members trading outside their committee jurisdiction with no demonstrated outperformance. Also: high-volume diversified traders with no sector concentration (noise). Treat as background unless clustered with Tier 1-2 signals.

The multiplier applies to the confidence model: a Tier 1 member's committee-relevant trade adds 3x the P_catalyst boost compared to a Tier 4 trade.

Additional evidence-based modifiers:
* Sell signal bias: All sell/short/put trades get 1.5x the weight of buy trades (Molk & Partnoy 2024).
* Party control: When the member's party controls their chamber, multiply signal by 1.25x (Wei/Zhou 2025).
* Spousal/proxy trades: Weight equally to member's own trades. Spousal trades of powerful members actually outperform the members' own trades (Karadas 2018) — possibly because spouses attract less scrutiny.
* Disclosure speed decay: 70-80% of alpha dissipates before disclosure (Ozlen & Batumoglu 2025). Fast disclosers (<10 days) retain more residual signal.
* Freshman watch: First-term members on key subcommittees should be monitored intensively for 12-24 months. They may not realize the scrutiny level and trade more aggressively (empirical: Fields, Shreve, Bresnahan in 2025).
* Retirement effect: Members who announce retirement may trade more aggressively with reduced concern for political consequences. Flag retiring members for elevated monitoring.

SIGNAL STRENGTH SCORING — UPDATED FEBRUARY 2026

Each congressional trade gets a composite signal score. Updated to incorporate findings from Molk & Partnoy (2024), Ozlen & Batumoglu (2025), Pyun (2025), and empirical 2025 data.

Core multipliers:

1. Member tier (Tier 1-4 multiplier as defined above) — 3x / 2x / 1x / 0.5x
2. Committee relevance (is the traded company in the member's committee jurisdiction?) — 2x if yes
3. Disclosure speed (disclosed within 7 days = 2x; 8-15 days = 1.5x; 16-30 days = 1.0x; 31-45 days = 0.5x; >45 days = 0.25x)
   Per Ozlen & Batumoglu (2025): 70-80% of alpha dissipates before disclosure. Fast disclosers retain the most signal.
4. Trade concentration (single stock vs. diversified buys) — concentrated = 1.5x
5. Pattern break (member rarely trades but made this one) — 2x
6. Watchlist category (Category 1-2 = 2x; Category 3-4 = 1.5x; Category 5 = 1.0x; not on watchlist = 0.75x)
7. Stock hasn't moved since trade date — 1.5x (information not yet priced in)
8. Bipartisan cluster (same-committee members of both parties trading same direction) — 2x

New evidence-based multipliers:

9. Sell/short bias — sells and put options get 1.5x the weight of buys (Molk & Partnoy 2024: sell signals outperform buy signals from Congress members)
10. Party control — member's party controls their chamber = 1.25x (Wei/Zhou 2025: returns are higher when the member's party has the majority)
11. Spousal/proxy trade — trade by spouse or family member of a powerful member = 1.25x (Karadas 2018: spousal trades of powerful members outperform even the member's own trades, likely because spouses attract less scrutiny and members route information-advantaged trades through them)
12. Legislative timing — trade occurred within 30 days before a vote the member participated in on related legislation = 2x (empirical: Bresnahan Medicaid sells, Crenshaw CARES Act buys, Mast cannabis buys, Greene tariff-pause buys)
13. Freshman burst — first-term member with >100 trades in first year on a key subcommittee = 1.5x (empirical: Fields, Shreve, Bresnahan in 2025 — freshmen may not yet realize scrutiny level)

Signal score = base_score × (tier_multiplier) × (committee_relevance) × (disclosure_speed) × (concentration) × (pattern_break) × (watchlist_category) × (price_not_moved) × (bipartisan_cluster) × (sell_bias) × (party_control) × (spousal_proxy) × (legislative_timing) × (freshman_burst)

Where base_score = 10, and only applicable multipliers are applied (default 1.0x for non-applicable).

Score interpretation:
* Score > 200: Critical signal. Extremely rare. Immediately cross-reference with value screen, insider buying data, and pending legislation. Example: a Tier 1 member selling a committee-relevant stock within 7 days of disclosure, before a legislative vote, while the stock hasn't moved — this scores 10 × 3 × 2 × 2 × 1.5 × 2 = 360.
* Score 100-200: Very high signal. Cross-reference with value screen and insider buying data. This trade likely reflects material non-public regulatory/legislative knowledge.
* Score 50-100: High signal. Add to active monitoring. Worth investigating the traded company.
* Score 20-50: Moderate signal. Log and watch for clustering with other signals.
* Score < 20: Low signal. Log but don't act. Likely routine portfolio management.

Weakest signals (discard or heavily discount):
* Mega-cap stock trades by diversified traders (everyone knows about Apple — but a concentrated bet in AAPL by a member who rarely trades IS a signal)
* High-frequency diversified traders with no sector concentration (noise traders like Tuberville: 1,263 trades across many sectors, poor performance)
* Trades disclosed at >45 days past the deadline (information is stale AND the member may be trying to hide the trade)
* Passive ETF/index fund rebalancing
* Members with documented poor track records (inverse signal may be worth exploring but not reliable enough to trade on)

Strongest signals to prioritize:
* Committee-relevant SELLS by Category 1-2 members disclosed within 7 days — the academically strongest signal type
* Pattern breaks: members with near-zero trading history who suddenly make a concentrated bet (Westerman 2025 model)
* Freshman bursts on key subcommittees (Fields 2025 model)
* Bipartisan committee clusters: when members of BOTH parties on the same committee trade the same direction in the same sector within 30 days
* Pre-legislative sells: members selling stocks in companies that would be harmed by legislation they're about to vote on (Bresnahan 2025 model)


LEGAL AND ETHICAL NOTES

* Congressional trading on non-public legislative information is technically prohibited by the STOCK Act but has never been meaningfully enforced.
* Tracking and acting on publicly disclosed congressional trades is completely legal for you. The disclosures are public records.
* The strategy is simply: observe what informed political actors do with their own money, and use that as one signal among many.
* This is no different than tracking corporate insider purchases (Form 4) — it's the same principle applied to political insiders.


WHAT TO WATCH OUT FOR

* Disclosure delays mean some trades are too old to act on by the time you see them. Focus on fast disclosers.
* The "Pelosi effect" means the most-watched members' trades are already crowded. The alpha is in the unwatched members.
* Members sometimes trade through spouses, trusts, or blind trusts. Spousal trades are still disclosed but get less attention — they can be more informative because the member is trying to be less visible.
* Legislative outcomes are uncertain. Even if a member buys a stock ahead of a bill they're drafting, the bill might not pass. This is probabilistic, not deterministic.
* Political risk: If a member's trading becomes a scandal, the stock could reverse if the narrative shifts to "this was insider trading" rather than "this company is doing well."
