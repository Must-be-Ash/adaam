Buffett's Playbook — If He Started Today With AI and Modern Data


THE SETUP

Buffett is 25 years old. It's 2026. He has no reputation, no Berkshire, no float, no network. He has a small amount of capital (say $500K), access to every public data source and API that exists, and an AI agent he can direct. The market is more efficient than the 1950s, more competitive, more algorithmic. But the edges he exploited — neglect, complexity, forced selling, patience — still exist in the same structural places, just harder to find manually.

Here's what he would do.


PRINCIPLE 1: NARROW THE UNIVERSE RUTHLESSLY

Buffett never screened the whole market. He picked one sector, learned it deeper than anyone, and lived there until he'd found every anomaly in it. He'd do the same today.

His starting universe:
Companies with market cap $50M–$500M. This is the zone where:
* Institutions can't operate meaningfully (too small for their fund size)
* Analyst coverage is sparse or zero (0-2 analysts)
* Algorithmic trading is thin (low liquidity = algos avoid it)
* Data quality is imperfect (creates information advantage for those willing to dig)

He would NOT look at large caps. He would NOT compete with quant funds on speed or data volume. He would go where the competition isn't.

Starting sector: Insurance.
He'd pick insurance again, because:
* The accounting is opaque (reserves, float, loss ratios) — creates judgment advantage
* The core asset (float) is invisible on standard screens
* Few AI systems are trained to evaluate insurance reserves properly
* The sector is cyclical — periodic hard markets create buying windows
* He understood it last time and the structural dynamics haven't changed

Second sector (after 12-18 months): Regional and community banks during the next stress cycle.
Same logic: opaque book values, depositor behavior requires judgment, cyclical stress creates forced selling.


PRINCIPLE 2: READ EVERYTHING THE MACHINES SKIP

In the 1950s, Buffett read every page of Moody's manuals. Today, the equivalent is the unstructured text in SEC filings that machines process poorly:

What his AI agent reads for him:
* Every 10-K for every company in the $50M-$500M universe (that's ~2,000 companies)
* Focus on: MD&A, risk factors, footnotes on reserves (insurance), loan loss provisions (banks), related-party transactions, auditor opinions
* Quarter-over-quarter language comparison: detect when management language shifts from specific to vague, from confident to hedging
* Footnote anomalies: one-time items, off-balance-sheet liabilities, changing accounting methods

What he reads personally:
* The shortlist. After the AI agent flags 30-50 companies with anomalous financials + interesting textual signals, Buffett reads those 10-Ks himself. Every footnote. Every proxy statement.
* He would say: "The AI gets me from 2,000 to 50. I get from 50 to 5."


PRINCIPLE 3: THE QUANTITATIVE SCREEN — SIMPLE, STRICT, UNCHANGED

His screening criteria today would be almost identical to the 1950s. The formulas haven't changed because the math of value hasn't changed.

Primary filters (must pass all):
* P/B < 1.0 (preferably < 0.7)
* Tangible P/B < 1.0 (exclude goodwill and intangibles)
* P/E < 8 (using normalized earnings, not reported)
* EV/EBIT < 6
* FCF yield > 12%
* No annual losses in the last 5 years (consistency)
* Interest coverage > 4x (safety)
* Net debt / EBITDA < 2.5 (balance sheet strength)

Secondary filters (must pass 2+ of these):
* ROE > 12% sustained over 5 years
* Insider ownership > 5%
* Insider buying in trailing 12 months
* Share count flat or declining over 5 years
* Dividend covered 3x+ (if paying a dividend)
* Piotroski F-Score > 6

He would not add complexity. He would not use machine learning to optimize thresholds. He would say: "If a company is genuinely undervalued, it'll be obvious on these numbers. If you need a fancy model to find the edge, the edge isn't real."


PRINCIPLE 4: INSIDER BUYING AS THE PRIMARY CONVICTION SIGNAL

This is the one thing Buffett would add to his 1950s toolkit, because the data didn't exist in accessible form back then.

Setup:
* Monitor every Form 4 filed daily across the $50M-$500M universe
* Flag cluster buying: 3+ insiders buying open-market shares within 90 days
* Weight CEO/CFO purchases higher than director purchases
* Cross-reference with the quantitative screen

His reasoning: "I want to own cheap businesses where the people running them are buying with their own money. If the CEO is putting $2M of his personal savings into the stock at a P/B of 0.6, he knows something I can verify but the market hasn't noticed."

This would be his highest-conviction filter. A company that passes the value screen AND has insider cluster buying goes to the top of the pile.


PRINCIPLE 5: THE KILL LIST IS THE REAL WORK

Buffett said no to 99% of what he looked at. His kill discipline would be even stricter today because there are more traps (zombie companies kept alive by cheap debt, accounting gimmicks, promotional management).

Automatic kills:
* Going concern opinion from auditor
* Negative tangible equity
* Negative FCF for 3+ consecutive years
* Accelerating dilution (share count growing > 5% annually)
* Auditor change + restatement in same 2-year period
* Revenue declining 4+ consecutive years in a structurally shrinking industry
* CEO compensation growing faster than every financial metric for 3+ years
* Net debt / EBITDA > 4 with maturities within 24 months

Manual review kills:
* Related-party transactions of any kind — he'd read every one and kill most
* Acquisition-driven growth with declining ROIC
* Management with no insider ownership and no purchases
* Complex multi-entity structures designed to obscure true economics


PRINCIPLE 6: CONGRESSIONAL AND POLITICAL SIGNALS — YES, HE'D USE THEM

Buffett has always operated in political circles. He advised Obama. He understands how regulation affects industries. Today, he'd systematically monitor congressional trading:

* Track Armed Services Committee members' trades → defense and government contractor positioning
* Track Finance Committee members' trades → banking and insurance regulation signals
* Track Health Committee members' trades → pharma and healthcare regulation signals
* Focus on committee chairs and ranking members (they have the most access)
* Weight bipartisan committee clusters highest (when both parties trade the same direction, the signal is non-partisan information, not political positioning)

He wouldn't copy-trade any individual politician. He'd use it as a catalyst signal — "If three Finance Committee members are buying insurance stocks and my value screen already flagged two insurance companies as cheap, that's convergence."


PRINCIPLE 7: BOND MARKET AS A SANITY CHECK

Buffett always understood credit. He'd use bond market signals as a validation layer, not a primary screen:

* For any company on the shortlist, check if it has traded debt
* If bonds are trading at 95+ cents on the dollar (healthy) but the stock is depressed → the bond market is telling you the company can pay its bills. Increases confidence.
* If bonds are deteriorating while the stock holds → warning signal. Something credit analysts see that equity investors don't.
* He would never trade the bond-equity arb directly. He'd just use it to confirm or deny his fundamental thesis.


PRINCIPLE 8: NEWS AND EVENTS — ONLY AS A TRIGGER, NOT A STRATEGY

Buffett would not build a social signal arbitrage system. He'd call it speculation. He doesn't predict earnings — he buys businesses he wants to own for decades.

But he would use news as a trigger for when to look:

* Sector panic (banks crash, insurance selloff, energy collapse) → "Let me check my universe, something just got cheaper"
* Company-specific stress (earnings miss, lawsuit, regulatory action) → "Was this company already on my watchlist? Has anything about the business actually changed?"
* Spin-off announcement → "New company about to be orphaned. Add to watchlist and monitor for forced selling."
* Post-bankruptcy emergence → "Clean balance sheet, forced selling by creditors. Worth reading the fresh-start 10-K."

He would not monitor Google Trends or TikTok. He'd say: "I don't need to know what consumers are doing this week. I need to know what a business will earn over the next 10 years."


PRINCIPLE 9: POSITION SIZING AND CONCENTRATION

Buffett would not diversify across 50 names. He'd concentrate:

* Maximum 8-12 positions
* Top 3 convictions get 20-25% each (the majority of capital)
* Remaining positions get 5-10% each
* Cash reserve of 15-25% at all times (dry powder for when something exceptional appears)

His sizing rule: "The size of the position should reflect the quality of the idea. If I'm right about a company and I only put 2% in it, I haven't really done anything. If I'm wrong, the kill criteria should have caught it."

He would scale into positions over weeks or months (small/micro-cap stocks can't absorb large buys without moving the price).


PRINCIPLE 10: HOLD PERIOD — YEARS, NOT MONTHS

Minimum hold: 2 years.
Target hold: 5-10 years.
Ideal: Forever, if the business compounds.

He would not trade around earnings. He would not take profits at +30%. He would hold through volatility because his original thesis was about the business, not the stock price.

Sell triggers:
* The original thesis is broken (business fundamentals deteriorated, not just price)
* Management changed for the worse
* Valuation reached fair value or above (P/B > 1.5, P/E > 15 for a slow grower)
* A better opportunity appeared and capital is needed
* A kill criterion that didn't exist at purchase now applies


THE DAILY ROUTINE

Morning (AI agent runs automatically):
* Ingest all Form 4s filed yesterday → flag insider clusters
* Ingest all 8-Ks filed yesterday → flag material events in the universe
* Check bond prices for any shortlisted company with traded debt
* Check for new congressional trade disclosures → flag committee-relevant trades
* Run valuation screen against the universe → flag any new companies crossing thresholds

Weekly (Buffett reviews):
* AI agent delivers a 1-page summary: new insider clusters, new screen hits, material news events, any kill criteria triggered on existing positions
* Buffett reads the shortlisted 10-Ks and 10-Qs that the AI flagged
* Updates conviction scores on existing positions

Quarterly:
* Full portfolio review against kill criteria
* Re-run the entire screen fresh to catch anything that drifted into value territory
* Read all earnings call transcripts for existing positions (AI pre-flags language changes)

Annually:
* Review all purchases from the prior year against outcomes
* Calibrate the confidence model (was the 70% confidence group actually right 70% of the time?)
* Adjust base rates and evidence weights based on empirical results


WHAT HE WOULD NOT DO

* He would not use leverage
* He would not short stocks (too much risk, limited upside, unlimited downside)
* He would not trade options (time decay works against you)
* He would not chase momentum or social signals
* He would not optimize for speed — his edge is patience
* He would not diversify away his best ideas
* He would not use machine learning models he can't explain in plain English
* He would not trade daily, weekly, or even monthly
* He would not care about quarterly performance — only multi-year compounding

His quote today would be: "Everyone has the same data now. Nobody has more patience. That's still the edge."


EXPECTED OUTCOMES

Year 1-2: Build the system, screen the universe, read 200+ 10-Ks, make 4-6 initial purchases. Portfolio likely flat or slightly up — not enough time for theses to play out.

Year 3-5: First positions start re-rating. Expect 2-3 positions to appreciate 80-200%. 1-2 positions to go sideways. 1 position to be a loss (kill criteria missed something or business deteriorated). Blended portfolio return: 15-25% annualized.

Year 5-10: Compounding effect kicks in. Winners are held, losers are cut. The system's calibration improves from empirical feedback. Concentrated positions in the best ideas drive returns. Target: 20-30% annualized, which over 10 years at 25% turns $500K into ~$4.6M.

Year 10+: At this scale, he'd start hitting the same problem he hit in real life — the portfolio gets too big for micro-caps. He'd migrate up to small-caps, then mid-caps, then (decades later) he'd be forced into large-caps and his returns would compress to market-like levels. But by then, the compounding has done its work.

The key insight: the strategy doesn't scale infinitely. It works precisely because it operates where big money can't. That's a feature, not a bug — it means the edge persists for as long as you stay small.
